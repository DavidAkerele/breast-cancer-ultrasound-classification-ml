\section{Introduction}
This chapter presents the empirical outcomes of the experimental investigations conducted in this dissertation. We systematically analyze multi-model diagnostic classification performance, multi-class confusion matrix error distributions, ROC and Precision-Recall curves, quantitative noise resilience across increasing speckle tiers ($\sigma \in [0.01, 0.15]$), biophysical SNR and CNR gains, aspect ratio distortion quantification, forward-pass prediction certainty distributions, semi-supervised pseudo-labeling confidence threshold ablations, and epoch-by-epoch training convergence dynamics.

\section{Experimental Setup and Computational Environment}
All models were implemented using PyTorch 2.x and torchvision, with biophysical preprocessing accelerated via OpenCV and NumPy. Model training and evaluation were executed on a dedicated high-performance workstation equipped with hardware-accelerated matrix multiplication engines (Apple Silicon Metal Performance Shaders / NVIDIA CUDA). To ensure rigorous reproducibility, all random number generators across Python, NumPy, and PyTorch were seeded with a fixed seed ($S = 42$).

\subsection{Clinical Risk Analysis of False Negatives}
In oncological screening, diagnostic errors carry asymmetric clinical costs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{False Positive (FP):} A benign lesion misclassified as malignant triggers follow-up imaging and tissue biopsy, causing transient patient anxiety and clinical expense.
    \item \textbf{False Negative (FN):} A malignant carcinoma misclassified as benign delays essential therapeutic intervention (chemotherapy, surgical resection), potentially allowing metastatic progression and resulting in fatal outcomes \citep{Shen2019}.
\end{itemize}

As demonstrated in Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices}, EfficientNet-B0 yields exceptional clinical safety, producing only \textbf{2 false negatives out of 80 malignant cases} ($97.5\%$ sensitivity), compared to 3 false negatives in ResNet-50 and 12 false negatives in the Custom CNN baseline.

\section{Aspect Ratio Geometric Distortion Quantification}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:aspect_ratio_distortion}, direct anamorphic resizing squishes rectangular clinical sonograms into fixed square tensors, introducing an average geometric distortion error of $\mathcal{AR} = +38.5\%$. In contrast, the Proposed ROI Reflection Square Padding algorithm enforces isotropic spatial scaling ($s_x = s_y$), achieving a mathematically verified $\mathcal{AR} = 0.0\%$ distortion error and preserving critical taller-than-wide malignant tumor margins.

\subsection{Tradeoff Analysis: Confirmation Bias vs. Data Starvation}
The empirical data in Table~\ref{tab:pseudo_label_ablation} reveals two fundamental statistical boundary regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Low Thresholds ($\tau \le 0.90$): Confirmation Bias.} Filtering candidates at $\tau = 0.80$ incorporates 520 pseudo-labeled scans; however, false positive candidate predictions introduce label noise into the student training stream, degrading precision (Macro F1 = $0.9661$).
    \item \textbf{High Thresholds ($\tau \ge 0.99$): Data Starvation.} Overly stringent filtering at $\tau = 0.99$ retains only 185 candidates, discarding valuable ambiguous boundary cases and causing student network gains to plateau prematurely (Macro F1 = $0.9749$).
    \item \textbf{Optimal Sweet Spot ($\tau = 0.95$):} Filtering at $\tau = 0.95$ achieves the optimal balance, retaining 350 high-quality pseudo-labeled scans and delivering the highest overall Macro F1 (\textbf{$0.9871$}), Malignant F1 (\textbf{$0.9910$}), and Macro AUC (\textbf{$0.9891$}).
\end{itemize}
